# Creativity: from correct to memorable

Language models collapse toward the most typical answer, and different models collapse toward the
same one. Avoiding the generated look only removes the most typical result; the next most typical
one is "tasteful and safe": correct, restrained, forgettable. A good designer gets past both by
starting from an idea, generating real alternatives, borrowing from outside the medium, and then
committing.

Use this in Create mode, and in Extend mode when the system leaves the identity open. Creativity
goes into identity, content and signature moments. Usability stays conventional (see `ux.md`):
people should be surprised by how it looks and feels, never by how it works.

## 1. Find the concept

Before colors and fonts, write the **concept**: one sentence that says what this design is, built
from something true about the subject. Every later decision should express it.

- Weak: "a modern, clean site for a climbing gym."
- Strong: "the gym's walls as a route map: every section is a problem with a grade, colors work like
  the tape that marks routes, and the page climbs from easy to hard."

Mine the subject for raw material (the research step helps): its objects, materials, tools, rituals,
vocabulary, places, history, what insiders notice that outsiders don't. Climbing has holds, chalk,
tape colors, grades, topo maps, route-setting, the send. Invoicing has stamps, ledgers, paper
stock, the moment of getting paid.

## 2. Diverge before choosing

Your first concept is the most probable one, so don't stop there.

- Write **five concepts** that span the whole range, from the obvious to the unusual, each with a
  one-line rationale and your honest estimate of how typical it is (how likely any designer, or any
  model, would land on it).
- Include at least one built on a **far analogy**: borrowed from outside the web, such as signage,
  packaging, a printed program, a map, a tool, an instrument panel, a game. Analogies that are far
  but still related raise novelty; ones that are too far stop helping, so keep a real link.
- Drop concepts that fight the brief or the users' job. From the rest, pick the **least typical one
  that still clearly fits**, or present two or three to the user (step 5 of the skill).

## 3. Commit with signature moves

A concept only shows when it changes something visible. Choose a few **signature moves** and make
them decisively; timid versions of good ideas read as defaults.

- **One structural move**: a layout, typographic system or navigation idea that comes from the
  concept (sections as graded routes, an index styled like a topo legend, numbers set as the hero).
- **One signature interaction**: the micro-interaction people remember (see `motion.md`).
- **Material**: real imagery, texture, illustration or data from the subject rather than stock and
  icons. Where there are no assets, design with type, color and shape so the absence doesn't show.
- **Contrast**: commit to clear extremes in scale, weight and density somewhere. Pages that keep
  every value in the middle feel generic even when every value is correct.

Everything else stays quiet so the signature moves carry. Restraint means fewer bold moves, not
smaller ones.

## 4. Check for ambition, not only correctness

After the plan check against the anti-patterns, ask:

- Could this design belong to any other gym, shop or app in its category? Then the concept isn't
  visible yet.
- What would someone screenshot or tell a friend about? If nothing, add or sharpen a signature move.
- Would a studio known for distinctive work show this in its portfolio? What would it change?
- Is anything bold only in the plan but timid in the build?

Then build, look at the result (screenshots where available), and push the one or two places that
fell flat. Remove one accessory that doesn't serve the concept.
